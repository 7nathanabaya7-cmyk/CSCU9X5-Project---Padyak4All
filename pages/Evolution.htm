The “Boneshaker”

The boneshaker was the first mass-produced bicycle with pedals, called a velocipede by its makers in the 1860s. Pierre Michaux and others bolted the cranks directly to the front wheel, so one pedal turn meant one wheel turn.

The frame was wood or wrought iron, and the wheels had iron or solid rubber tires with no suspension. Every bump went straight into the rider, which gave the bike its name. Speed was capped by wheel diameter, a limit that led to the penny-farthing.

The “Safety Bicycle”

In 1885, John Kemp Starley’s Rover put a chain on the rear wheel. Gearing no longer depended on wheel size, so both wheels could be the same height and the rider sat low and balanced.

The triangulated diamond frame was light and strong. Dunlop’s air-filled tire (1888) then cut rolling resistance and vibration. This layout is still the standard today.

The “Gear Shift”

Hub gears came first, with the Sturmey-Archer three-speed in 1902. Derailleurs followed, moving the chain across several sprockets so riders could hold a steady cadence on climbs, flats and descents.

Modern groupsets run 12 speeds or more, and electronic shifting such as Shimano Di2 and SRAM eTap uses small motors to move the derailleur.

The “Material Shift”

Frames moved from steel to aluminum in the 1970s, then to carbon composites. Carbon’s advantage is that engineers can tune stiffness by direction: stiff sideways for power transfer, compliant vertically for comfort.

It can also be molded into aerodynamic tube shapes that metal tubing can’t easily match.

The “Disc Brake”

Early bikes used a spoon brake that pressed a pad against the tire. Caliper rim brakes took over from the 1890s, and hydraulic disc brakes appeared on mountain bikes in the 1990s before reaching road bikes around 2015.

Because braking force no longer runs through the rim, stopping power stays consistent in the wet and is easier to control.

The “Smart Bike”

Power meters, GPS head units, Strava and smart trainers turned riding into measurable data: watts, FTP and cadence. Training moved from feel and heart rate to numbers.

E-bikes added a battery and torque-sensing motor assist, widening who can ride and how far.
